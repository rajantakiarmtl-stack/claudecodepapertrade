# Build pre-trade checks on filings, not prices

The $0-licensing plan works if the product is built on **U.S. government filings and statistics** and does not depend on market data. SEC EDGAR, BLS, BEA, Treasury, the Fed Board, CFTC, EIA, openFDA, ClinicalTrials.gov, GDELT and Statistics Canada can be stored, transformed and shown to paying users, subject to rate limits and attribution. FINRA short data, Cboe statistics, Nasdaq Trader files, OPRA options data, every free stock-price API tier, Yahoo-derived mirrors such as DefeatBeta, FRED's copyrighted series, congressional STOCK Act reports, and Canada's SEDAR+/SEDI cannot. Several of the sources the original plan called "public domain" sit on that second list. The founder cannot get stock prices for free. The legal zero-cost route is to show charts in TradingView widgets and buy a display license (Tiingo, about **$250/mo**) as soon as revenue arrives. All of this data can go into one master database keyed on an internal `security_id`, with point-in-time identifier history, bitemporal timestamps, and a license class on every row so restricted data never reaches a screen. The strongest first product is a per-ticker "pre-trade check" led by two features: **dilution risk** and **event-in-window warnings** (earnings, FDA and macro dates before the trade's planned exit). Both rest almost entirely on green-light EDGAR and agency data, both match documented retail pain, and no competitor found combines them. Expect willingness to pay of about **$15–$29/mo**. The best public benchmark, Quiver Quantitative, converts only about **0.7%** of registrations, so 1,000 payers out of 5,000–10,000 total users is far above category norms. Caveat: the research tools could not fetch most primary terms pages directly. Every verdict below comes from search excerpts and has to be confirmed against live terms, and the high-risk items need a lawyer.

## Federal filings are green, market-structure data is mostly red

The legal split runs between government-authored or government-mandated disclosure, which is reusable, and data compiled by private SROs, exchanges and vendors, which comes with contract terms. Copyright is rarely the binding constraint. Closing prices and filing facts are facts, and *Feist* protects only selection and arrangement ([ESA Feist summary](https://www.esa.int/About_Us/Law_at_ESA/Intellectual_Property_Rights/Feist_ruling_-_USA)). The binding constraint is the contract you accept when you download from a website or API. **FINRA**, for example, limits its website content "ONLY for your own non-commercial personal or professional use" ([FINRA Terms of Use](https://www.finra.org/terms-of-use)). Its API Equity Data terms allow redistribution only for end users' "non-commercial personal or professional use" and state that "End Users shall not be permitted to further redistribute" ([FINRA Specific Terms for Equity Data](https://developer.finra.org/specific-terms-equity-data)). **Cboe** allows "one copy of the Materials for your personal non-commercial use" ([Cboe Terms](https://www.cboe.com/terms)). That covers its put/call statistics and VIX history CSVs. **FRED** is a Federal Reserve Bank service, not a federal agency, and its terms bar commercial redistribution of third-party series without the owner's written permission ([FRED Legal Notices](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/legal/)). FRED is therefore usable only for series without "Copyright" in their notes, which FRED says can be found by searching ([FRED API Terms](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/docs/api/terms_of_use.html)). The safer design is to pull public-domain series from the originating agency (BLS, BEA, Treasury, the Fed Board) rather than from FRED.

The catalog below consolidates verdicts for a paid, display-to-subscribers product. **Green** means commercial storage, transformation and display appear permitted, with conditions such as rate limits and attribution. **Yellow** means unclear, conditional, or paid-but-affordable. **Red** means prohibited without a license, or legally high-risk.

The Attorney General can bring civil actions, and OGE lists an inflation-adjusted penalty cap of **$25,132** ([Senate Ethics](https://www.ethics.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/financialdisclosure); [OGE](https://www.oge.gov/web/oge.nsf/Officials%20Individual%20Disclosures%20Search%20Collection?OpenForm=)). Quiver, Capitol Trades and Unusual Whales display this data. The research found no source explaining how they justify it under 13107 and no DOJ enforcement against trackers, so their presence in the market is not legal clearance. **A Canadian founder cannot cover Canadian issuers for free**, because SEDAR+ and SEDI need an ASC license. StatCan and probably the Bank of Canada are the only clean Canadian sources.

## No free price feed survives a paid product, so widgets come first and Tiingo is the first spend

Every mainstream free price tier checked restricts use to personal, internal or non-commercial purposes. Alpaca content is "exclusively for personal and noncommercial access and use" ([Alpaca Terms copy](https://zesty-public-documents.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/alpaca_agreement.pdf)). Massive (formerly Polygon) personal plans are "solely for your own personal, non-commercial, and non-business purposes" ([Massive Individuals ToS](https://polygon.io/individuals-terms-of-service)). Tiingo's free and Power plans are "for internal and personal use only" ([Tiingo docs](https://www.tiingo.com/documentation/)). Alpha Vantage is "personal, non-commercial" unless agreed in writing ([Alpha Vantage ToS](https://www.alphavantage.co/terms_of_service/)). Stooq forbids redistribution without consent ([Stooq terms](https://stooq.com/terms.html)). The yfinance maintainers say Yahoo's API "is intended for personal use only" ([yfinance PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/yfinance/)). Finnhub reportedly requires written approval to redistribute even "derived results" ([ZeroChart PR #109](https://github.com/blackhebrewisraeli/ZeroChart/pull/109)). Computing an earnings-reaction percentage on a free key and showing only that number therefore does not escape the contract.

Exchange policy is less restrictive than vendor contracts. The UTP Plan treats delayed data as carrying "no charge" on controlled products. It also treats derived data that "cannot be reverse engineered" and is calculated "upon multiple UTP security symbols" as not fee-liable ([UTP Data Policies](https://www.utpplan.com/DOC/Datapolicies.pdf)). Vendor summaries say "end-of-day data isn't fee liable in the US" ([Quodd](https://www.quodd.com/exchange-agreements)). In practice, though, the data arrives under a vendor's terms, and those terms are what restrict its use. The only free bulk source whose terms appear to contemplate redistribution is **IEX HIST**: free T+1 downloads with required "Data provided for free by IEX" attribution ([IEX HIST terms](https://www.iex.io/legal/hist-data-terms); [IEX developer](https://iextrading.com/developer/)). It covers only trades on the IEX venue and needs pcap parsing, so bars built from it will not match official consolidated closes.

The recommended zero-cost strategy has four parts. First, **show prices and charts only through TradingView widgets** with attribution left intact. TradingView serves the data inside its own iframe, so the product never receives or stores it. TradingView's policies require that attribution not be altered ([TradingView Policies](https://www.tradingview.com/policies/)). Second, **design the MVP so that no check depends on stored price data**: dilution, insider activity, filings-based catalysts and estimated earnings windows need none. Third, if a price-derived check is essential before revenue, compute it from IEX HIST and label it "IEX-venue prices", or use Tiingo's explicit carve-out for software where users supply their own API token, "provided the software is not distributing our data" ([Tiingo docs](https://www.tiingo.com/documentation/)). The BYO-key model only works client-side with no server caching. That makes cross-user precomputation impossible and adds friction, so treat it as a stopgap. Fourth, avoid yfinance, Stooq, DefeatBeta, and the free tiers of Alpaca, Massive and Alpha Vantage in production. That includes the Alpha Vantage tools available in this research environment.

The **first paid upgrade is Tiingo's Base Equities Redistribution package at $250/mo for startups**. It covers EOD prices, IEX-derived real-time data, corporate actions and a security master ([Tiingo EOD](https://www.tiingo.com/products/end-of-day-stock-price-data); [Tiingo real-time](https://www.tiingo.com/products/consolidated-real-time-stock-api)). An independent analysis also found Tiingo to be the one provider with a published display license at that price ([fantasy-stock-app PR](https://github.com/giorgiofatica/fantasy-stock-app/pull/380)). At 1,000 payers this costs about $0.25 per user per month. A **realistic trigger is the first 20–50 paying users** rather than 1,000, because price-derived checks (earnings-move history, realized volatility, gap risk) rank among the highest-value features. The alternatives are less clear. Twelve Data's reported external-display tier is about $499/mo, from secondary sources ([wisesheets](https://blog.wisesheets.io/best-stock-and-financial-data-apis/)). Databento's equities bundles are "free to license for distribution, display, and non-display" in exchange-fee terms, but only "with any active subscription plan", and its own vendor display terms are unconfirmed ([Databento blog](https://databento.com/blog/dbeq-basic)). Intrinio's Startup tier advertises "Commercial Use and Display Rights" at an unpublished price ([Intrinio](https://intrinio.com/pricing)). Marketstack's $9.99 "Commercial Use" plan could be the cheapest option, but its display scope is unknown, so ask Marketstack in writing. Whatever the vendor, get written confirmation that the license covers display to paying subscribers, storage, caching, derived metrics and charts.

Options data has a hard floor. OPRA requires a vendor agreement even for delayed data and charges a **$1,500/mo** redistribution fee (**$650/mo** query-only) "whether on a current or delayed basis". Only historical-only redistribution is exempt ([OPRA fee schedule](https://cdn.opraplan.com/documents/OPRA_Fee_Schedule.pdf); [OPRA](https://www.opraplan.com/)). One vendor claims delayed data carries no fee ([Theta Data](https://www.thetadata.net/articles/2026-05-29-opra-fee-guide-for-options-market-data)), but OPRA's own wording supports the fee. Until revenue justifies that cost, options checks should use proxies built from licensed equity prices: realized volatility and the historical distribution of earnings-day moves, clearly labeled "not market implied volatility".

## One master database can hold everything if license and time travel with every row

The sources normalize cleanly, because almost everything hangs off two identifiers: the SEC **CIK** for issuers and filers, and the **ticker** for listed securities. Neither is stable enough to be a primary key. Tickers get reused and changed, and one CIK can have several share classes. The schema should use an internal surrogate `security_id` and `issuer_id`, and map external identifiers through a validity-dated history table. One identifier needs care. Nasdaq's spec says distributors cannot receive files containing **CUSIP** data "unless they have a license in place with CUSIP" ([Nasdaq Daily List spec](https://www.nasdaqtrader.com/content/technicalsupport/specifications/dataproducts/dlcompletespec.pdf)). CUSIPs should therefore never be displayed, and should not be stored unless a licensed vendor provides them. FinanceDatabase (MIT, 300k+ symbols) can seed sector and classification mappings, but its upstream data provenance needs checking before display ([FinanceDatabase](https://github.com/JerBouma/FinanceDatabase)).

| Table | Key | Core columns | Point-in-time handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| `source` | `source_id` | name, terms_url, terms_checked_at, license_class (GREEN/YELLOW/RED), allow_store, allow_display, allow_derived_display, attribution_text, rate_limit | Re-verify terms on a schedule; changing a class re-gates every dependent row |
| `issuer` | `issuer_id` | legal name, CIK, country, SIC, filer status | Name and SIC history kept in `issuer_attr_history` |
| `security` | `security_id` | issuer_id, class, type (common/ADR/ETF/warrant), listing status | Delisted securities are never deleted, which keeps statistics free of survivorship bias |
| `identifier_history` | (security_id, id_type, valid_from) | id_type (ticker, CIK, exchange MIC), value, valid_to, source_id | Resolves "what was ticker X on date D"; handles ticker reuse |
| `filing` | `accession_no` | cik, form_type, items[], period_of_report, **accepted_at** (SEC acceptance timestamp), amends_accession, url | `accepted_at` is the knowledge time for everything parsed from the filing |
| `insider_txn` | (accession_no, line_no) | owner, role, txn_date, code (P/S/A/M/F/G), shares, price, post_holdings, is_10b5_1, footnotes | Knowledge time = Form 4 acceptance, **not** txn_date; 4/A rows supersede rather than overwrite |
| `financial_fact` | (issuer_id, concept, unit, period_end, period_start, accession_no) | value, fy, fp, frame, filed_at | Bitemporal: the period is valid time, `filed_at` is knowledge time; restatements are added as new rows; queries "as known on date D" pick the latest `filed_at ≤ D` |
| `offering_event` | `event_id` | issuer_id, type (S-3 effective, 424B5, ATM agreement, S-1/F-1, warrant/convert), amount, accession_no | Shelf capacity is computed as of a date, never as current state |
| `ownership_position` | (filer_cik, security_id, report_period, accession_no) | shares, value, form (13F/13D/13G/N-PORT) | Report period vs. filing date (13F lag); unit changes normalized |
| `calendar_event` | `event_id` | security_id or macro, event_type (earnings, AdCom, PDUFA, trial completion, macro release, ex-div), event_date, **date_status** (confirmed/estimated), method, confidence, source_id | Estimates and confirmations are stored separately; both are kept |
| `price_bar_daily` | (security_id, date, source_id) | OHLCV, adj_factor | YELLOW class; empty until licensed |
| `corporate_action` | `action_id` | split, dividend, symbol change, ex_date | Needed to adjust prices and remap identifiers |
| `macro_observation` | (series_id, obs_date, vintage_date) | value, source_id | Vintages capture revisions (BLS/BEA revise; FRED vintages via ALFRED) |
| `check_result` | (security_id, check_type, as_of) | verdict, explanation, metrics JSON, **sample_size**, input_row_refs, derived_license_class, computed_at | Reproducible: stores exactly which knowledge-time snapshot produced it |

Two design rules make the "restricted data never reaches display" requirement enforceable rather than aspirational. First, **every fact row carries `source_id` and an ingestion timestamp**, and every computed row records the sources it used. Its `derived_license_class` is set to the most restrictive class among them, like a taint flag that spreads to anything computed from restricted input. The application then reads only from views filtered on `allow_display` or `allow_derived_display`, never from base tables. A RED-tainted metric can still be used internally (for example, to test whether a signal is worth licensing), but the display layer cannot see it. Second, **knowledge time is a column, not an afterthought**. EDGAR's Form 4 acceptance timestamp, XBRL `filed` dates and 13F filing dates decide when a fact became public. A backtest such as "stocks with insider cluster buys returned X% over 30 days" has to key on acceptance time, or it uses information traders did not yet have. The edgartools library already standardizes XBRL statements, derives Q4 from the 10-K, and handles SEC fair access ([edgartools](https://github.com/dgunning/edgartools)). That makes much of this ingestion close to off-the-shelf. The design rule is never to overwrite: store amendments and restatements as new rows that supersede old ones, and resolve them at query time.

Honest statistics follow from the same structure. Every price-dependent or event-study output should show its **sample size and dispersion**: "median earnings-day move 6.1%, range 1–14%, n = 12 reports" rather than a bare average. It should also refuse to show a verdict below a minimum n. The DIY checklists traders already use contain no such discipline ([AI Driven Trading Substack](https://aidriventrading.substack.com/p/an-10x-high-risk-stock-model-i-follow)). The documented 1,000x revenue misread in Perplexity's finance tool ([HelmTerminal](https://helmterminal.dev/blog/perplexity-stock-research)) shows why deterministic, source-linked numbers are a selling point.

### Closing the earnings-calendar gap from EDGAR (inference)

Earnings dates are the most valuable input with no clean free source. The Nasdaq earnings calendar is graded tier D, "terms prohibit automated access", by the global-stock-data project ([global-stock-data](https://github.com/simonlin1212/global-stock-data)). Yahoo- and Nasdaq-scraping calendar libraries are stale and inherit those sites' terms ([yahoo-earnings-calendar](https://github.com/wenboyu2/yahoo-earnings-calendar); [finance_calendars](https://github.com/s-kerin/finance_calendars)). The popular OhEarningsCal project has no license ([OhEarningsCal](https://github.com/jason5ng32/OhEarningsCal)). Earnings Whispers sells institutional calendar data on a "contact us" basis ([EW institutional](https://www.earningswhispers.com/institutional-data)). Free EDGAR data supports a derived estimate instead. Each CIK's filing index in the submissions API lists 8-K item numbers, and edgartools supports item-level 8-K search ([edgartools](https://github.com/dgunning/edgartools)). From that history the product can pull the **dates of past 8-K Item 2.02 ("Results of Operations") filings**, the item companies use for earnings releases. Their fiscal-quarter cadence then gives an estimated window for the next report. For example: "Company reported within days 22–26 after quarter end in each of the last 8 quarters → next report estimated Jan 21–25, confidence high." The 10-Q/10-K filing dates, and the gap between them and the 8-K, refine the estimate. These are background-knowledge inferences about form semantics and should be validated against a sample. This approach produces estimates, not confirmed dates. It should label them that way, store them with `date_status = estimated`, and upgrade to "confirmed" only from a licensed source or from the company's own announcement. For an event-in-window check, a well-calibrated window ("earnings likely falls before your expiry") still answers most of the trader's question.

## Reuse MIT, Apache and CC0 code; learn from AGPL designs

The open-source ecosystem has commoditized SEC parsing, XBRL fundamentals, Form 4 and 13F handling, short-volume charts and MCP tool wrappers. Those should be reused, not rebuilt. The gaps are maintained congressional ingestion (the original Stock Watcher projects are stale), FTD dashboards, biotech catalyst calendars, permissively licensed earnings calendars, packaged event-study tooling, and above all a synthesized per-ticker checklist. A GitHub search for PDUFA or biotech catalyst trackers returned no repositories above 3 stars. The main constraint on reuse is that **a code license is not a data license**. A repo can be MIT-licensed while scraping data whose terms forbid commercial use.

The UX patterns worth copying recur across these projects. Equibles uses a per-ticker hub with one tab per dataset. LuxAlgo shows a visible data-health and staleness board. global-stock-data attaches plain-English caveats to every metric, for example that "daily short volume counts that day's short-sale trades, not open short positions". Equibles' paid cloud tier is a useful market signal: it charges for transcripts, options and Greeks, guidance, and **buyback and ATM programs**. That suggests dilution tracking built from free EDGAR filings is something vendors consider monetizable.

## Competitors sell single dimensions at $20–$50 a month; nobody sells the checklist

The market has four layers. Free government-data mirrors include OpenInsider, Capitol Trades and WhaleWisdom's free tier. Alt-data and community tools cost $10–$30/mo. Active-trader tools cost $40–$80/mo. Pro terminals start above $100/mo. Every product profiled scores one dimension: Fintel's 0–100 Short Squeeze Score ([Fintel](https://fintel.io/ss/us/fdva)), DilutionWatch's 0–100 dilution score ([DilutionWatch](https://dilutionwatch.com/)), Danelfin's AI score. Reviewers criticize those scores as "proprietary, not fully documented" ([Tapeboard](https://tapeboard.pages.dev/compare/fintel-alternative)). A targeted search found no product combining dilution, catalysts, insider activity, short data and earnings-reaction history into one per-ticker pre-trade view.

**Quiver** is the most useful benchmark. By mid-2025 it reported about **$2M ARR, 750,000 registered users and more than 5,000 users paying about $30/mo**, with 75% of revenue from web subscriptions ([IB Madison](https://ibmadison.com/doubling-down-on-data/)). That is roughly 0.7% conversion from registration to paid. Generic freemium apps convert 2–5% ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/freemium-app-monetization-strategies/)), and hard paywalls reach a 12.1% median ([ContentGrip](https://www.contentgrip.com/subscription-app-growth-playbook/)). The founder's milestone of 1,000 payers within 5,000–10,000 total users implies 10–20% conversion. That is possible only with a hard paywall or a very targeted audience. At Quiver-like rates, 1,000 payers takes about 140,000 registrations. This matters for the licensing plan: holding prices at $0 "until 1,000 payers" could mean years without price-derived features. That is another reason to buy the $250/mo license early.

Competitors acquire users mainly through **programmatic per-ticker SEO pages**. Examples are Quiver's `/congresstrading/stock/{TICKER}` ([Quiver RIVN](https://www.quiverquant.com/congresstrading/stock/RIVN)), Fintel's `/ss/us/{ticker}` pages in several languages, and Unusual Whales' landing pages for "vs" comparisons and reviews ([UW vs InsiderFinance](https://unusualwhales.com/lp/unusual-whales-vs-insiderfinance)). Alerts are the clearest dividing line between free and paid tiers. Capitol Trades lacks alerts, and reviewers flag it ([DayTradeReview](https://daytradereview.com/insider-alerts-review)). Unusual Whales' free tier is useful but late. A pre-trade tool should copy both: free, indexable per-ticker pages, with alerts and watchlist sweeps behind the paywall.

## Dilution and event-window checks should ship first; congress and options flow should wait

Retail pain is best documented in two places. The first is **unnoticed earnings inside an options trade**: IV crush, "right on direction, still down", and max losses from forgotten earnings weeks ([ApexVol](https://apexvol.com/avoid/earnings-trading-mistakes); [Paragon Signals](https://buttondown.com/Paragon-Signals/archive/you-were-right-and-still-lost-money/); [saltwaterbrc blog](https://saltwaterbrc-net.pages.dev/blog/nok-earnings-lesson)). The second is **buying small caps just before ATM or shelf dilution**. Traders currently check that by hand: S-3 filings from the past three years, shelf size against market cap, 424B5 filings, "at-the-market" agreement language, and the baby-shelf one-third-of-float limit when public float is under $75M ([Curved Trading checklist](https://curvedtrading.com/articles/en/how/how-to-spot-dilution-sec-filings/); [Curved Trading ATM guide](https://curvedtrading.com/articles/en/investing/atm-offerings-shelf-registrations/)). That manual process can be automated almost entirely from green-light EDGAR data, so it is where the $0 constraint and willingness to pay overlap.

The MVP should ship ranks 1, 2, 3 and 6 on a single per-ticker page. All are built from EDGAR and agency data and need no price license. A traffic-light summary sits at the top, followed by drill-down tabs, a source link and "data as of" badge on each check, and a TradingView widget for the chart. The trader enters a ticker and, optionally, a planned exit or expiry date. That date makes the event-window check concrete without asking about positions or account size. Ranks 4 and 5 are the second release, timed to the Tiingo purchase. Ranks 7–10 should wait until revenue justifies licensing, and congressional data may never be worth the legal exposure.

The **free tier** should be the full per-ticker check page for any ticker, with checks delayed a day and limited history. It doubles as the programmatic SEO surface ("{TICKER} dilution risk", "{TICKER} next earnings estimate"). The **paid tier** (about **$19/mo or $179/yr**, inside the $15–$29 band where research tools cluster) adds real-time filing updates, watchlist sweeps, email and push alerts when any flag flips (new 424B5, Form 4 sale, earnings window narrowing, threshold-list entry), ICS calendar export, full history, and the price-derived checks once licensed. Annual-led pricing and first-year discounts are the category norm. TipRanks has no monthly plan, and Quiver offers 50% off the first year ([Quiver CCJ](https://www.quiverquant.com/congresstrading/stock/CCJ)). A later **$39–$49 tier** becomes viable only once licensed options or short data is added, since that is where Unusual Whales and SecForm4 price. At 1,000 payers and $19, the product grosses about $19k MRR, against roughly $250–$500/mo of data cost before any OPRA or FINRA licensing.

## Legal exposure concentrates in congress data, personalization and unread terms

The data risks are already flagged in the catalog. Using STOCK Act reports commercially carries statutory civil exposure. FINRA, Cboe, Nasdaq and the vendor terms are contract risks, and contract risk does not depend on whether the underlying facts are copyrightable. SEDAR+ and SEDI prohibit both scraping and commercial redistribution. The securities-law risk depends on product design. Following the earlier review's framing (not independently sourced in these notes), impersonal per-ticker research published to everyone fits the U.S. *Lowe v. SEC* publisher exclusion and Canada's NI 31-103 advising-generally exemption far better than outputs tailored to a user's portfolio. The per-ticker check, with a user-entered date and no position or account data, should be the core. The "portfolio sweep" should be framed as a watchlist of tickers rather than holdings with sizes. Every popular project in the space carries prominent "educational/research only" disclaimers ([ai-hedge-fund](https://github.com/virattt/ai-hedge-fund)). A founder in Québec also has to design for **Law 25** privacy obligations from day one: minimal collection, especially of anything resembling holdings or broker connections. Verdict wording matters too. "Dilution flags: 3 of 5 triggered" is research. "Don't buy" starts to look like advice.

The research tools could not fetch most primary terms pages: finra.org, cboe.com, opraplan.com, nasdaqtrader.com, sec.gov, fred.stlouisfed.org, tiingo.com, alpaca.markets and sedarplus.ca all failed. Every verdict above therefore rests on search-engine excerpts, secondary sources or background knowledge. The items below have to be checked against live pages or with counsel before launch.

## Conclusion

The founder's advantage is not access to data competitors lack. It is willingness to do the laborious part properly: point-in-time EDGAR parsing, transparent rules, sample-size-honest statistics, and license gating built into the schema. The free data that is legally clean happens to cover the two most painful pre-trade failures, getting diluted and getting caught by an event, while the data that is legally dirty (short interest, options flow, congressional trades) is also the most commoditized and is offered free elsewhere. A $0-licensing launch is therefore a positioning choice as well as a constraint. Lead with "filing-grounded, source-linked risk flags" against black-box scores and hallucinating AI assistants.

Two numbers should change the plan. The license fee that unlocks the most valuable deferred features is about $250/mo, the price of roughly thirteen subscriptions at $19. The category's conversion rate means 1,000 payers is a long way off. The practical rule is to stay at $0 only until the first few dozen subscribers, then buy display rights and ship the price-derived checks. Spend the legal budget on one securities-and-privacy opinion covering *Lowe*, NI 31-103 and Law 25 before launch, because that, not data licensing, is the risk that could shut the product down.
